1What is Living?

📝 Note

The current NCERT Chapter 1 (2026–27 reprint) starts directly with diversity and taxonomy. “What is living?” is still in the NEET 2026 syllabus, so the key points (from the earlier NCERT edition) are given here for completeness.

Living organisms show certain characteristics. Some are defining (true of all living things and no non-living thing), others are not.

CharacteristicDefining feature?Explanation
GrowthNoLiving things grow by cell division (increase in mass and number). But non-living things like mountains, sand mounds and crystals also “grow” by accumulation of material on the surface. In living things, growth is from inside.
ReproductionNoMany organisms do not reproduce: mules, sterile worker bees, infertile human couples. So reproduction cannot be an all-inclusive defining feature.
MetabolismYesThe sum of all chemical reactions in the body. No non-living object shows metabolism. Metabolic reactions can be shown outside the body in cell-free systems (in vitro), but these are neither living nor non-living — they are living reactions.
Consciousness (response to stimuli)YesAll organisms sense their environment and respond to physical, chemical or biological stimuli. Humans are the only organisms aware of themselves (self-consciousness).
Cellular organisationYesAll living things are made of cells.

📌 Remember

Metabolism, consciousness and cellular organisation are the defining properties of life. Growth and reproduction are NOT defining features. Patients in a coma (brain-dead) on life support are an example discussed with respect to consciousness.

2Biodiversity & the Need for Classification

2.1 Biodiversity

  • Each different kind of plant, animal or organism represents a species.
  • The number of species known and described is between 1.7 and 1.8 million. This number/variety of organisms on Earth is called biodiversity.
  • New organisms are continuously being identified as we explore new and old areas.

2.2 Why do we need to classify and name organisms?

  • Local names vary from place to place even within a country, which causes confusion.
  • Names must be standardised so one organism has the same name everywhere. This is nomenclature.
  • Nomenclature is possible only when the organism is correctly described and we know which organism the name refers to. This is identification.
  • Since it is impossible to study all organisms one by one, we group them into convenient categories based on easily observable characters. This is classification.
  • The scientific term for these categories is taxa (singular: taxon). Examples: “Plants”, “Animals”, “Mammals”, “Dogs”, “Wheat” — all are taxa at different levels.

3Taxonomy and Systematics

3.1 Taxonomy

  • The process of classifying organisms into different taxa based on characteristics is taxonomy.
  • Basis of modern taxonomic studies: external and internal structure, structure of cell, development process and ecological information.
  • The four basic processes of taxonomy: characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature.
  • The earliest classifications were based on the uses of organisms (food, clothing, shelter).

3.2 Systematics

  • The study of different kinds of organisms, their diversity and the relationships among them.
  • Word derived from the Latin systema — systematic arrangement of organisms.
  • Linnaeus used Systema Naturae as the title of his publication.
  • Scope of systematics was later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification. Systematics takes into account evolutionary relationships between organisms.

🎯 Exam Tip

Taxonomy = characterisation + identification + classification + nomenclature. Systematics = taxonomy + evolutionary relationships (phylogeny). “Systematics” is the broader term.

4Binomial Nomenclature

  • Codes for scientific names: Plants — ICBN (International Code for Botanical Nomenclature); Animals — ICZN (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature).
  • Scientific names ensure that each organism has only one name, and that the name has not been used for any other organism.
  • Binomial nomenclature was given by Carolus Linnaeus. Each name has two parts: the generic name and the specific epithet.
  • Example: Mango — Mangifera indica. Mangifera = genus, indica = specific epithet.

4.1 Rules of nomenclature (frequently tested)

  1. Biological names are generally in Latin and written in italics. They are Latinised or derived from Latin irrespective of their origin.
  2. The first word is the genus; the second is the specific epithet.
  3. When handwritten, both words are separately underlined; when printed, they are in italics.
  4. The genus starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet starts with a small letter.
  5. The name of the author appears after the specific epithet, in abbreviated form. Example: Mangifera indica Linn. — means the species was first described by Linnaeus.

⚠️ Common Mistake

“Mangifera Indica” is WRONG (capital I). “Mangifera indica” is correct. When handwritten, underline “Mangifera” and “indica” separately, not as one continuous line.

5Taxonomic Categories & Hierarchy

  • Classification is not a single step but a hierarchy of steps. Each step represents a rank or category.
  • Since each category is part of the overall taxonomic arrangement, it is called a taxonomic category, and all categories together form the taxonomic hierarchy.
  • Each category is a unit of classification and is called a taxon. Taxonomic groups are distinct biological entities, not merely morphological aggregates.
  • Obligate categories in ascending order: Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum (Division in plants) → Kingdom.
  • Species is the lowest category for all organisms.

5.2 Trends in the hierarchy

  • Going up from species to kingdom, the number of common characteristics decreases.
  • Lower the taxon, more characteristics the members share.
  • Higher the category, greater the difficulty of determining relationships with other taxa at the same level — classification becomes more complex at higher levels.
  • Taxonomists have also developed sub-categories (e.g., sub-species, sub-family) for more precise placement.

6Five Kingdom Classification

6.1 History of classification systems

  • Aristotle — earliest scientific attempt. Classified plants into trees, shrubs and herbs (simple morphological characters). Animals into those with red blood and those without.
  • Linnaeus — Two Kingdom system: Plantae and Animalia.
  • Drawbacks of the two kingdom system: did not distinguish eukaryotes from prokaryotes, unicellular from multicellular, or photosynthetic (green algae) from non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms.
  • Earlier systems grouped bacteria, blue-green algae, fungi, mosses, ferns, gymnosperms and angiosperms under “Plants” only because they all had a cell wall.
  • R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the Five Kingdom Classification: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
  • Criteria used by Whittaker: cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogenetic relationships.
  • A three-domain system has also been proposed. It divides Monera into two domains, leaving the eukaryotic kingdoms in the third domain — giving a six kingdom classification.

🔑 Key Point

Why fungi were separated: fungi are heterotrophic with chitin walls, while green plants are autotrophic with cellulose walls. Kingdom Protista brought together Chlamydomonas and Chlorella (earlier algae/plants, with cell walls) with Paramoecium and Amoeba (earlier animals, without cell walls).

7Kingdom Monera

  • Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera.
  • Most abundant micro-organisms; found almost everywhere — hundreds in a handful of soil; also in extreme habitats like hot springs, deserts, snow and deep oceans. Many are parasites.

7.1 Shapes of bacteria

SphericalCoccus / cocci
Rod-shapedBacillus / bacilli
Comma-shapedVibrio / vibrio
SpiralSpirillum / spirilla

7.2 Nutrition

  • Bacteria as a group show the most extensive metabolic diversity.
  • Autotrophic — photosynthetic or chemosynthetic autotrophs (make food from inorganic substrates).
  • Heterotrophic — vast majority; depend on other organisms or dead organic matter.

7.3 Archaebacteria

Live in the most harsh habitats: Halophiles (extreme salty areas), Thermoacidophiles (hot springs), Methanogens (marshy areas). They differ from other bacteria in having a different cell wall structure, responsible for survival in extreme conditions. Methanogens live in the gut of ruminants (cows, buffaloes) and produce methane (biogas) from dung.

7.4 Eubacteria (“true bacteria”)

Have a rigid cell wall and, if motile, a flagellum.

(a) Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae): have chlorophyll a similar to green plants; photosynthetic autotrophs. Unicellular, colonial or filamentous; freshwater, marine or terrestrial. Colonies surrounded by a gelatinous sheath; often form blooms in polluted water. Some fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts, e.g., Nostoc and Anabaena.

(b) Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria: oxidise inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites and ammonia and use the released energy for ATP production. Important in recycling nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and sulphur.

(c) Heterotrophic bacteria: most abundant in nature; majority are important decomposers. Useful: making curd from milk, production of antibiotics, nitrogen fixation in legume roots. Harmful: pathogens causing cholera, typhoid, tetanus, citrus canker.

7.5 Reproduction in bacteria

Mainly by fission. Under unfavourable conditions, they produce spores. A sort of sexual reproduction occurs by a primitive type of DNA transfer from one bacterium to another.

7.6 Mycoplasma

Completely lack a cell wall; the smallest living cells known; can survive without oxygen; many are pathogenic in animals and plants.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Cyanobacteria are called “blue-green algae” but they are NOT algae — they are prokaryotes in Kingdom Monera. Heterocysts are the nitrogen-fixing cells of cyanobacteria (Nostoc, Anabaena), not of true algae.

8Kingdom Protista

  • All single-celled eukaryotes are placed under Protista. Boundaries are not well defined.
  • NCERT includes five groups: Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds and Protozoans.
  • Primarily aquatic; forms a link with plants, animals and fungi.
  • Being eukaryotes, they have a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Some have flagella or cilia.
  • Reproduce asexually and sexually (by cell fusion and zygote formation).

8.1 Chrysophytes

Include diatoms and golden algae (desmids). Freshwater and marine; microscopic; float passively as plankton; most are photosynthetic. Diatoms: cell walls form two thin overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box, embedded with silica (indestructible). Accumulated diatom walls = diatomaceous earth, used in polishing and filtration of oils and syrups. Diatoms are the chief producers in the oceans.

8.2 Dinoflagellates

Mostly marine and photosynthetic; appear yellow, green, brown, blue or red depending on pigments. Cell wall has stiff cellulose plates. Most have two flagella — one longitudinal, one transverse in a furrow. Red dinoflagellates (e.g., Gonyaulax) multiply rapidly causing red tides; released toxins may kill fish and marine animals.

8.3 Euglenoids

Mostly freshwater, found in stagnant water. No cell wall; instead a protein-rich layer called pellicle makes the body flexible. Two flagella — one short, one long. Photosynthetic in sunlight; behave as heterotrophs (mixotrophic) when deprived of light. Pigments identical to higher plants. Example: Euglena.

8.4 Slime moulds

Saprophytic protists; body moves along decaying twigs and leaves engulfing organic matter. Under suitable conditions form an aggregation called plasmodium (may spread over several feet). Under unfavourable conditions, form fruiting bodies bearing spores; spores have true walls, are resistant, survive for years and are dispersed by air currents.

8.5 Protozoans

All are heterotrophs — predators or parasites; believed to be primitive relatives of animals.

📌 Remember — protist one-liners

Soap-box silica wall = diatoms. Red tide = Gonyaulax. Pellicle = Euglena. Plasmodium (aggregation) = slime moulds. Gullet = Paramoecium. Sleeping sickness = Trypanosoma. Do not confuse “plasmodium” (slime mould stage) with “Plasmodium” (malarial parasite).

9Kingdom Fungi

9.1 General features

  • Unique kingdom of heterotrophic organisms; great diversity in morphology and habitat.
  • Seen on moist bread and rotten fruits; mushrooms and toadstools are fungi. White spots on mustard leaves — parasitic fungus (Albugo). Yeast (unicellular) — bread and beer. Puccinia — wheat rust. Penicillium — source of antibiotics.
  • Cosmopolitan; prefer warm and humid places (hence refrigeration prevents spoilage).
  • Except yeasts (unicellular), fungi are filamentous. Body: thread-like hyphae; network of hyphae = mycelium. Coenocytic hyphae — continuous, multinucleate, no cross walls. Septate hyphae — with cross walls (septae).
  • Cell wall made of chitin and polysaccharides.
  • Nutrition: Saprophytes (most fungi, absorb from dead substrates), Parasites (on living plants/animals), Symbionts (with algae as lichens; with roots as mycorrhiza).

9.2 Reproduction in fungi

  • Vegetative: fragmentation, fission and budding.
  • Asexual: spores called conidia, sporangiospores or zoospores.
  • Sexual: oospores, ascospores and basidiospores, produced in fruiting bodies. Three steps: (1) Plasmogamy — fusion of protoplasms; (2) Karyogamy — fusion of nuclei; (3) Meiosis in the zygote → haploid spores.
  • In ascomycetes and basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryotic stage (n+n) — the dikaryophase — occurs before nuclei fuse.
  • Basis of dividing fungi into classes: morphology of mycelium, mode of spore formation, and fruiting bodies.

10Kingdom Plantae & Kingdom Animalia (overview)

Kingdom Plantae

  • All eukaryotic chlorophyll-containing organisms (plants).
  • A few are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous plants — Bladderwort and Venus fly trap; parasite — Cuscuta.
  • Cells have prominent chloroplasts and a cellulose cell wall.
  • Includes algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms.
  • Life cycle alternates between a diploid sporophyte and haploid gametophyte — alternation of generations.

Kingdom Animalia

  • Heterotrophic, eukaryotic, multicellular; cells lack cell walls.
  • Depend directly or indirectly on plants for food; digest food in an internal cavity; store food as glycogen or fat. Nutrition is holozoic (by ingestion).
  • Definite growth pattern; adults have a definite shape and size.
  • Higher forms show elaborate sensory and neuromotor mechanisms; most are capable of locomotion.
  • Sexual reproduction by copulation of male and female, followed by embryological development.

11Viruses, Viroids, Prions & Lichens

These are not included in Whittaker's five kingdom classification.

11.1 Viruses

Not considered truly “living” if living means having a cell structure — they are non-cellular with an inert crystalline structure outside the living cell. Once inside a cell, they take over the host machinery to replicate, killing the host. “Virus” means venom or poisonous fluid.

ScientistContribution
Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892)Recognised microbes causing mosaic disease of tobacco; found them smaller than bacteria (passed through bacteria-proof filters)
M.W. Beijerinck (1898)Showed extract of infected tobacco could infect healthy plants; named the pathogen “virus” and the fluid Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid)
W.M. Stanley (1935)Showed viruses could be crystallised; crystals consist largely of proteins

Viruses are obligate parasites; inert outside the specific host cell. Contain protein + genetic material (RNA or DNA) — no virus contains both RNA and DNA. A virus is a nucleoprotein; the genetic material is infectious.

HostUsual genetic material
Plant virusesSingle-stranded RNA
Animal virusesSingle- or double-stranded RNA, or double-stranded DNA
Bacteriophages (bacterial viruses)Usually double-stranded DNA

The protein coat is the capsid, made of subunits called capsomeres, arranged helically or polyhedrally, protecting the nucleic acid. Viral diseases in humans: mumps, small pox, herpes, influenza, AIDS. Symptoms in plants: mosaic formation, leaf rolling/curling, yellowing and vein clearing, dwarfing and stunted growth. Key examples: TMV (Tobacco Mosaic Virus, RNA virus) and Bacteriophage (head, collar, sheath, tail fibres).

11.2 Viroids

Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971). Infectious agent smaller than viruses; caused potato spindle tuber disease. A free RNA lacking the protein coat (hence “viroid”), with low molecular weight.

11.3 Prions

Infectious agents made of abnormally folded protein; similar in size to viruses. Cause infectious neurological diseases: Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) — “mad cow disease” in cattle; Creutzfeldt–Jacob disease (CJD) — the analogous variant in humans.

11.4 Lichens

Symbiotic (mutually useful) associations between algae and fungi. Algal partner = phycobiont (autotrophic); fungal partner = mycobiont (heterotrophic). Algae prepare food for fungi; fungi provide shelter and absorb minerals and water. Lichens are very good pollution indicators — they do not grow in polluted areas (sensitive to SO2).

🎯 Exam Tip

Virus = protein + nucleic acid (RNA or DNA). Viroid = only RNA (no protein coat). Prion = only protein (no nucleic acid). This three-way comparison is a favourite NEET question.

Fungi and the members of Monera and Protista that have cell walls are now excluded from Plantae. So cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) are not “algae” any more.

13Algae

13.1 General features

Chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic, largely aquatic (fresh water and marine). Also on moist stones, soils and wood; with fungi (lichen) and with animals (e.g., on sloth bear). Form and size vary: colonial (Volvox), filamentous (Ulothrix, Spirogyra); marine kelps form massive plant bodies.

13.2 Reproduction in algae

Vegetative: fragmentation. Asexual: spores, most commonly zoospores (flagellated, motile). Sexual: fusion of two gametes.

13.3 Economic importance of algae

  • At least half of the total CO2 fixation on Earth is by algae through photosynthesis; they increase dissolved oxygen and are primary producers for aquatic food cycles.
  • Food: ~70 species of marine algae used as food, e.g., Porphyra, Laminaria, Sargassum.
  • Hydrocolloids: algin (brown algae) and carrageen (red algae). Agar — from Gelidium and Gracilaria (red algae); used to grow microbes and in ice-creams and jellies.
  • Chlorella — unicellular alga rich in proteins; food supplement even for space travellers.

13.5 Chlorophyceae (green algae)

Plant body unicellular, colonial or filamentous. Grass green due to dominant chlorophyll a and b in definite chloroplasts (discoid, plate-like, reticulate). Cell wall: inner layer cellulose, outer layer pectose. Reproduction: fragmentation; asexual by flagellated zoospores; sexual isogamous, anisogamous or oogamous. Examples: Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Ulothrix, Spirogyra, Chara.

13.6 Phaeophyceae (brown algae)

Primarily marine; range from simple branched filaments (Ectocarpus) to profusely branched kelps (up to 100 metres). Colour olive-green to brown from xanthophyll fucoxanthin. Food stored as laminarin or mannitol; cellulosic wall covered by gelatinous algin. Plant body: holdfast, stipe and frond. Asexual reproduction by pear-shaped biflagellate zoospores. Examples: Ectocarpus, Dictyota, Laminaria, Sargassum, Fucus.

13.7 Rhodophyceae (red algae)

Red due to r-phycoerythrin. Mostly marine, more in warmer areas; found near the surface and at great depths. Food: floridean starch (similar to amylopectin/glycogen). Vegetative: fragmentation; asexual: non-motile spores; sexual: non-motile gametes, oogamous. Examples: Polysiphonia, Porphyra, Gracilaria, Gelidium.

📌 Remember — algae examples by class

Green: Chlamydomonas, Volvox, Ulothrix, Spirogyra, Chara. Brown: Ectocarpus, Dictyota, Laminaria, Sargassum, Fucus. Red: Polysiphonia, Porphyra, Gracilaria, Gelidium. Porphyra, Laminaria, Sargassum = food. Algin = brown. Carrageen and agar = red.

14Bryophytes

14.1 General features

  • Include mosses and liverworts; grow in moist shaded areas in the hills.
  • Called “amphibians of the plant kingdom” — live on soil but depend on water for sexual reproduction. Important in plant succession on bare rocks/soil.
  • Plant body thallus-like, prostrate or erect; attached by unicellular or multicellular rhizoids. Lack true roots, stem and leaves.
  • Main plant body is haploid — the gametophyte (dominant phase). Sex organs are multicellular: Male — antheridium → biflagellate antherozoids; Female — archegonium (flask-shaped), produces a single egg.
  • Antherozoids released into water reach the archegonium; fusion with egg → zygote → multicellular sporophyte, which is not free-living — attached to and nourished by the gametophyte. Some sporophyte cells undergo meiosis → haploid spores → germinate into gametophytes.

14.2 Economic and ecological importance

Some mosses are food for herbaceous mammals, birds and other animals. Sphagnum (moss) provides peat — used as fuel and as packing material (due to its water-holding capacity). Mosses with lichens are the first organisms to colonise rocks (pioneers), decomposing rock to prepare substrate for higher plants, and form dense mats that prevent soil erosion.

⚠️ Common Mistake

In bryophytes the gametophyte is dominant and independent; the sporophyte is dependent on it. In pteridophytes and seed plants, the sporophyte is dominant. Gemmae are asexual (not spores, not gametes).

15Pteridophytes

15.1 General features

  • Include horsetails and ferns; used as medicine, soil-binders and ornamentals.
  • First terrestrial plants to possess vascular tissues — xylem and phloem.
  • Main plant body is the sporophyte — true root, stem and leaves with vascular tissue. Leaves: microphylls (small, e.g., Selaginella) or macrophylls (large, ferns).
  • Sporangia are subtended by leaf-like sporophylls, which may form compact strobili/cones (Selaginella, Equisetum).
  • Spores (from meiosis) germinate into free-living, photosynthetic, thalloid gametophytes called prothallus, needing cool, damp, shady places — restricting pteridophytes to narrow geographical regions because of the water needed for fertilisation.
  • Gametophytes bear antheridia and archegonia; water carries antherozoids to the archegonium → zygote → multicellular sporophyte (dominant phase).

15.2 Homospory and heterospory

  • Homosporous: all spores of one kind (majority of pteridophytes).
  • Heterosporous: two kinds of spores — megaspores (large) and microspores (small). Examples: Selaginella and Salvinia.
  • Megaspores → female gametophyte; microspores → male gametophyte. Female gametophyte is retained on the parent sporophyte; zygote develops into a young embryo within it — a precursor to the seed habit, an important evolutionary step.

📊 PYQ Trend

Heterospory in Selaginella and Salvinia and its role as the “precursor of seed habit” is one of the most repeated NEET questions from this chapter.

16Gymnosperms

16.1 General features

  • Gymnos = naked, sperma = seeds. Ovules are not enclosed by any ovary wall and remain exposed before and after fertilisation — seeds are naked.
  • Medium or tall trees and shrubs; giant redwood Sequoia is one of the tallest tree species.
  • Roots generally tap roots: Pinus has fungal association as mycorrhiza; Cycas has specialised coralloid roots with N2-fixing cyanobacteria.
  • Stems unbranched (Cycas) or branched (Pinus, Cedrus). Conifers have needle-like leaves, thick cuticle and sunken stomata to reduce water loss.

16.2 Reproduction

  • Heterosporous — microspores and megaspores in sporangia on sporophylls forming strobili/cones.
  • Male strobili bear microsporangia → pollen grains (highly reduced male gametophyte). Female strobili bear megasporophylls with ovules.
  • Monoecious in Pinus (both cones on same tree); dioecious in Cycas (on different trees).
  • Megaspore mother cell in the nucellus → meiosis → 4 megaspores → one develops into a multicellular female gametophyte with two or more archegonia, retained within the megasporangium.
  • Male and female gametophytes have no independent free-living existence. Pollination by wind; pollen tube carries male gametes to archegonia → zygote → embryo; ovule → seed (not covered).

🔑 Key Point

Examples in NCERT figures: Cycas, Pinus, Ginkgo. Other names in text: Sequoia, Cedrus. Gymnosperms do NOT need water for fertilisation — the pollen tube delivers the male gametes.

17Angiosperms (brief)

  • Pollen grains and ovules develop in specialised structures called flowers; seeds are enclosed in fruits.
  • Very large group with a wide range of habitats. Size range: smallest Wolffia to tall Eucalyptus trees (over 100 metres).
  • Provide food, fodder, fuel, medicines and commercial products. Two classes: dicotyledons and monocotyledons.

18Animal Kingdom — Basis of Classification

Over a million species of animals have been described. Common fundamental features used for classification: arrangement of cells, body symmetry, nature of coelom, and patterns of digestive, circulatory or reproductive systems.

18.1 Levels of organisation

LevelDescriptionExample
CellularCells arranged as loose aggregates; some division of labourSponges (Porifera)
TissueCells performing the same function arranged into tissuesCoelenterates (and Ctenophores)
OrganTissues grouped to form organsPlatyhelminthes
Organ systemOrgans form functional systemsAnnelids, Arthropods, Molluscs, Echinoderms, Chordates (and Aschelminthes)
  • Digestive system: Incomplete — single opening for mouth and anus (Platyhelminthes). Complete — separate mouth and anus.
  • Circulatory system: Open type — blood pumped out of the heart, bathing cells/tissues directly. Closed type — blood circulates through vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries).

18.2 Symmetry

Asymmetrical — no plane divides into equal halves (most sponges). Radial — any plane through the central axis divides into identical halves (coelenterates, ctenophores, echinoderms). Bilateral — identical left and right halves in only one plane (annelids, arthropods, etc.).

18.3 Diploblastic and triploblastic

Diploblastic: two germ layers — ectoderm and endoderm, with mesoglea between (coelenterates, ctenophores). Triploblastic: third germ layer mesoderm between ectoderm and endoderm (Platyhelminthes to chordates).

18.4 Coelom

Coelom = body cavity between body wall and gut wall, lined by mesoderm. Coelomates: annelids, molluscs, arthropods, echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates. Pseudocoelomates: body cavity not lined by mesoderm, e.g., Aschelminthes. Acoelomates: body cavity absent, e.g., Platyhelminthes.

18.5 Segmentation

Body externally and internally divided into segments with serial repetition of organs = metameric segmentation (metamerism), e.g., earthworm. First seen in Annelida.

18.6 Notochord

Mesodermally derived rod-like structure on the dorsal side during embryonic development. Animals with a notochord = chordates; without = non-chordates (Porifera to Echinodermata, plus Hemichordata).

19Non-chordate Phyla

19.1 Phylum Porifera (sponges)

Generally marine, mostly asymmetrical; primitive multicellular animals with cellular level organisation. Water enters through ostia → central cavity spongocoel → exits through osculum (feeding, respiration, waste removal). Choanocytes (collar cells) line the spongocoel. Digestion intracellular. Skeleton of spicules or spongin fibres. Hermaphrodite; internal fertilisation, indirect development. Examples: Sycon (Scypha), Spongilla (freshwater sponge), Euspongia (bath sponge).

19.2 Phylum Coelenterata (Cnidaria)

Aquatic, mostly marine; sessile or free-swimming; radially symmetrical. Named from cnidoblasts/cnidocytes with stinging nematocysts (anchorage, defence, prey capture). Tissue level, diploblastic. Central gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening on the hypostome. Digestion extracellular and intracellular. Corals have a calcium carbonate skeleton. Two body forms: polyp (sessile, e.g., Hydra) and medusa (free-swimming, e.g., Aurelia). Alternation of generations (metagenesis) in Obelia: polyps produce medusae asexually, medusae form polyps sexually. Other examples: Physalia, Adamsia (sea anemone), Pennatula, Gorgonia, Meandrina (brain coral).

19.3 Phylum Ctenophora

Commonly sea walnuts or comb jellies; exclusively marine, radially symmetrical, diploblastic, tissue level. Eight rows of ciliated comb plates for locomotion. Bioluminescence well-marked. Hermaphrodite; only sexual reproduction, external fertilisation, indirect development. Examples: Pleurobrachia, Ctenoplana.

19.4 Phylum Platyhelminthes (flatworms)

Dorso-ventrally flattened; mostly endoparasites. Bilateral, triploblastic, acoelomate, organ level. Hooks and suckers in parasitic forms. Flame cells — osmoregulation/excretion. Internal fertilisation; many larval stages. Planaria shows high regeneration. Examples: Taenia (tapeworm), Fasciola (liver fluke).

19.5 Phylum Aschelminthes (roundworms)

Body circular in cross-section; free-living or parasitic. Organ-system level, bilateral, triploblastic, pseudocoelomate. Complete alimentary canal with muscular pharynx. Excretory pore removes wastes. Sexes separate; females often longer (sexual dimorphism). Examples: Ascaris, Wuchereria (filaria worm), Ancylostoma (hookworm).

19.6 Phylum Annelida

Aquatic or terrestrial; organ-system, bilateral, triploblastic, metamerically segmented, coelomate (Latin annulus = little ring). Longitudinal and circular muscles for locomotion. Nereis has parapodia for swimming. Closed circulatory system; nephridia for excretion. Double ventral nerve cord. Nereis is dioecious; earthworms and leeches are monoecious. Examples: Nereis, Pheretima (earthworm), Hirudinaria (leech).

19.7 Phylum Arthropoda

Largest phylum of Animalia — over two-thirds of all named species are arthropods. Organ-system, bilateral, triploblastic, segmented, coelomate. Chitinous exoskeleton; body: head, thorax, abdomen; jointed appendages. Respiration: gills, book gills, book lungs or tracheae. Open circulatory system. Sense organs: antennae, compound/simple eyes, statocysts. Excretion via Malpighian tubules. Mostly dioecious, oviparous.

CategoryExamples
Economically important insectsApis (honey bee), Bombyx (silkworm), Laccifer (lac insect)

19.8 Phylum Mollusca

Second largest animal phylum. Body covered by a calcareous shell; unsegmented, with head, muscular foot and visceral hump. Mantle covers the hump; mantle cavity contains feather-like gills. Mouth has a rasping radula. Usually dioecious, oviparous, indirect development. Examples: Pila (apple snail), Pinctada (pearl oyster), Sepia (cuttlefish), Loligo (squid), Octopus, Aplysia, Dentalium, Chaetopleura (chiton).

⚠️ Common Mistake

Cuttlefish (Sepia), devil fish (Octopus), silverfish and starfish are NOT fishes. Cuttlefish and devil fish are molluscs; starfish is an echinoderm.

19.9 Phylum Echinodermata

Endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles — “spiny bodied”. All marine; organ-system level. Adults radially symmetrical, larvae bilaterally symmetrical. Triploblastic, coelomate. Mouth on lower (ventral) side, anus on upper (dorsal) side. Distinctive: water vascular system (locomotion, food capture, respiration). Excretory system absent. Sexes separate; usually external fertilisation, indirect development with free-swimming larva. Examples: Asterias (star fish), Echinus (sea urchin), Antedon, Cucumaria, Ophiura.

19.10 Phylum Hemichordata

Earlier a sub-phylum of Chordata; now a separate phylum under non-chordata. Has a rudimentary stomochord (similar to notochord) in the collar region. Worm-like marine animals; body: proboscis, collar and trunk. Open circulatory system; respiration through gills; excretory organ = proboscis gland. Examples: Balanoglossus, Saccoglossus.

  2. Dorsal hollow nerve cord

20.3 Subphyla of Chordata

  • Urochordata (Tunicata) and Cephalochordata = protochordates; exclusively marine. Urochordata: notochord only in the larval tail, e.g., Ascidia, Salpa, Doliolum. Cephalochordata: notochord persists head to tail throughout life, e.g., Branchiostoma (Amphioxus/lancelet).
  • Vertebrata: notochord present in the embryo, replaced by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column in adults. All vertebrates are chordates, but all chordates are not vertebrates. Vertebrates also have a ventral muscular heart (2, 3 or 4 chambers), kidneys, and paired appendages (fins or limbs).

21Classes of Vertebrates

21.1 Class Cyclostomata

All living members are ectoparasites on some fishes. Elongated body with 6–15 pairs of gill slits; sucking, circular mouth without jaws; no scales or paired fins. Cranium and vertebral column cartilaginous; closed circulation. Marine but migrate to fresh water for spawning, dying soon after. Examples: Petromyzon (lamprey), Myxine (hagfish).

21.2 Class Chondrichthyes (cartilaginous fishes)

Marine, streamlined, cartilaginous endoskeleton. Mouth ventral; notochord persistent throughout life. Gill slits separate, without operculum. Placoid scales; teeth are modified placoid scales. No air bladder → must swim constantly. Two-chambered heart. Electric organs in Torpedo; poison sting in Trygon. Cold-blooded. Males have claspers; internal fertilisation; many viviparous. Examples: Scoliodon (dog fish), Pristis (saw fish), Carcharodon (great white shark), Trygon (sting ray).

21.3 Class Osteichthyes (bony fishes)

Marine and freshwater; bony endoskeleton; streamlined; mouth mostly terminal. Four pairs of gills covered by an operculum. Cycloid/ctenoid scales. Air bladder present — regulates buoyancy. Two-chambered heart; cold-blooded. External fertilisation; mostly oviparous, direct development. Examples: marine — Exocoetus (flying fish), Hippocampus (sea horse); freshwater — Labeo, Catla, Clarias; aquarium — Betta, Pterophyllum.

21.4 Class Amphibia

Amphi = dual, bios = life — aquatic and terrestrial habitats. Usually two pairs of limbs. Skin moist, without scales; eyes have eyelids; tympanum represents the ear. Digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts open into a cloaca. Respiration by gills, lungs and skin. Three-chambered heart (2 auricles, 1 ventricle); cold-blooded. External fertilisation, oviparous, indirect development. Examples: Bufo (toad), Rana (frog), Hyla (tree frog), Salamandra, Ichthyophis (limbless amphibian).

21.5 Class Reptilia

Latin repere = to creep/crawl; mostly terrestrial. Dry, cornified skin with epidermal scales/scutes. No external ear openings. Heart usually three-chambered, four-chambered in crocodiles. Poikilothermous; snakes/lizards shed skin as a cast. Internal fertilisation, oviparous, direct development. Examples: Chelone (turtle), Testudo (tortoise), Calotes (garden lizard), Crocodilus, Hemidactylus (wall lizard); poisonous snakes Naja (cobra), Bangarus (krait), Vipera.

21.6 Class Aves (birds)

Presence of feathers; most fly except flightless birds (e.g., ostrich); possess a beak. Forelimbs modified into wings. Skin dry, without glands except the oil gland at the tail base. Endoskeleton fully ossified; bones hollow/pneumatic. Digestive tract has crop and gizzard. Heart completely four-chambered; warm-blooded. Air sacs supplement respiration. Internal fertilisation, oviparous, direct development. Examples: Corvus (crow), Columba (pigeon), Psittacula (parrot), Struthio (ostrich), Pavo (peacock), Aptenodytes (penguin), Neophron (vulture).

21.7 Class Mammalia

Found in all habitats. Most unique feature: mammary glands. Two pairs of limbs for walking, running, climbing, burrowing, swimming or flying. Skin has hair; external ears (pinnae) present; heterodont teeth. Four-chambered heart; warm-blooded; lungs. Internal fertilisation; viviparous with few exceptions; direct development. Oviparous: Ornithorhynchus (platypus). Viviparous: Macropus (kangaroo), Pteropus (flying fox), Camelus, Macaca, Rattus, Canis, Felis, Elephas, Equus, Delphinus, Balaenoptera (blue whale), Panthera tigris, Panthera leo.

22Quick Revision — High-Yield One-liners

  • Number of described species: 1.7–1.8 million.
  • Binomial nomenclature: Linnaeus. Codes: ICBN (plants), ICZN (animals).
  • Lowest taxonomic category: species. Plants use Division instead of phylum.
  • Five kingdom classification: R.H. Whittaker (1969).
  • Chitin cell wall: Fungi. Non-cellulosic wall: Monera.
  • Methane from cattle dung: methanogens (archaebacteria).
  • Heterocysts: Nostoc, Anabaena.
  • Smallest living cells; no cell wall: Mycoplasma.
  • Diatomaceous earth: diatoms (silica walls).
  • Red tides: Gonyaulax (dinoflagellate).
  • Pellicle, mixotrophic: Euglena.
  • Sleeping sickness: Trypanosoma. Malaria: Plasmodium.
  • Dikaryophase: Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes.
  • Aseptate coenocytic mycelium: Phycomycetes.
  • Imperfect fungi: Deuteromycetes — Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma.
  • Neurospora: biochemical and genetic work.
  • Viruses named by Beijerinck; crystallised by Stanley; TMV mosaic by Ivanowsky.
  • Viroid (free RNA): T.O. Diener, 1971, potato spindle tuber disease.

  • Lichens: phycobiont + mycobiont; pollution indicators.
  • Algin: brown algae. Carrageen: red algae. Agar: Gelidium, Gracilaria.
  • Food supplement for space travellers: Chlorella.
  • Floridean starch: red algae. Laminarin/mannitol: brown algae.
  • Amphibians of plant kingdom: bryophytes. Peat: Sphagnum.
  • Gemma cups: Marchantia (liverwort). Protonema: mosses.
  • First vascular plants: pteridophytes. Heterosporous: Selaginella, Salvinia.
  • Coralloid roots with cyanobacteria: Cycas. Mycorrhiza: Pinus.
  • Tallest gymnosperm: Sequoia. Smallest angiosperm: Wolffia. Tall angiosperm: Eucalyptus.
  • Largest animal phylum: Arthropoda. Second largest: Mollusca.
  • Living fossil: Limulus.
  • Metagenesis: Obelia. High regeneration: Planaria.
  • Stomochord: Hemichordata.
  • Notochord only in larval tail: Urochordata; head to tail throughout life: Cephalochordata.
  • Electric organ: Torpedo. Poison sting: Trygon.
