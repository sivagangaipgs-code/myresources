Why does glare vanish through polarized sunglasses at just the right angle?

At one particular angle of incidence, light reflected off a surface becomes completely polarized. That special angle is called Brewster's angle — and the rule that predicts it is Brewster's Law.

Getting Started

What is Brewster's Law?

Brewster's Law states that when unpolarized light strikes the boundary between two transparent media at a particular angle — called the polarizing angle or Brewster's angle (ip) — the reflected ray is completely plane polarized, with vibrations only perpendicular to the plane of incidence. At this angle, the reflected and refracted rays are exactly perpendicular to each other. The law is written as tan ip = n₂ / n₁, where n₁ and n₂ are the refractive indices of the first and second media.

Why is this important?

Brewster's Law, discovered by Sir David Brewster in 1815, is one of the clearest demonstrations that light behaves as a transverse wave. It underlies polarizing sunglasses, camera filters, glare-reduction coatings, and the Brewster windows used in gas laser tubes to produce a clean, linearly polarized beam with minimal loss. It also appears frequently in board and competitive physics exams as both a conceptual and numerical topic.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this interactive lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what Brewster's angle is and why the reflected ray becomes fully polarized there
  • Identify the perpendicularity condition between reflected and refracted rays

  • Apply the law to find an unknown refractive index from a measured polarizing angle
  • Analyze how the reflected light's polarization changes as the angle of incidence varies
  • Connect the law to real devices such as polarizing filters and Brewster windows

    Key Facts

    • Discovered by Sir David Brewster, 1815
    • Only the reflected ray is completely polarized; the refracted ray is only partially polarized
    • Reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular exactly at ip
    • Air–Water ≈ 53.1°, Air–Glass ≈ 56.3°, Air–Diamond ≈ 67.5°
    • Applications: polarizing sunglasses, camera filters, laser Brewster windows
