1Morphology of Flowering Plants — Introduction

  • Angiosperms are characterised by the presence of roots, stems, leaves, flowers and fruits.
  • The underground part is the root system; the part above ground is the shoot system.
  • Morphology = study of external form. Standard technical terms and definitions are needed to classify and understand plants, and to understand adaptations (for protection, climbing, storage, etc.).

2The Root

2.1 Types of root systems

TypeOriginExample
Tap root systemDirect elongation of the radicle forms the primary root, which bears lateral roots (secondary, tertiary). Primary root + branches = tap root system. Common in dicots.Mustard
Fibrous root systemIn monocots, the primary root is short-lived and replaced by many roots arising from the base of the stem.Wheat
Adventitious rootsRoots arising from parts of the plant other than the radicle.Grass, Monstera, banyan tree

2.2 Functions of roots

  • Absorption of water and minerals from the soil
  • Proper anchorage to the plant
  • Storing reserve food material
  • Synthesis of plant growth regulators

2.3 Regions of the root tip (from apex upwards)

  1. Root cap — thimble-like structure covering the apex; protects the tender apex as it moves through soil.
  2. Region of meristematic activity — a few mm above the root cap; cells very small, thin-walled, dense protoplasm; divide repeatedly.
  3. Region of elongation — cells undergo rapid elongation and enlargement; responsible for growth in length.
  4. Region of maturation — cells differentiate and mature. Some epidermal cells here form root hairs, which absorb water and minerals.

2.4 Modifications of root [Beyond current NCERT]

🎯 Exam Tip

Sweet potato is a root (adventitious root modified for storage). Potato is a stem (tuber). This pair is a classic NEET trap.

3The Stem

3.1 Features

  • Ascending part of the axis bearing branches, leaves, flowers and fruits.
  • Develops from the plumule of the embryo.
  • Has nodes (where leaves are borne) and internodes (portions between nodes).
  • Bears buds — terminal or axillary.
  • Green when young, later often woody and dark brown.
  • Features that distinguish stem from root: nodes and internodes, multicellular hairs and positively phototropic nature.

3.2 Functions

  • Spreading out branches bearing leaves, flowers and fruits.
  • Conducting water, minerals and photosynthates.
  • Some stems: storage of food, support, protection, vegetative propagation.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Thorn (stem modification, arises from axillary bud, has vascular connection) vs spine (leaf modification, e.g., cactus) vs prickle (epidermal outgrowth, e.g., rose). NEET tests which one arises from an axillary bud.

4The Leaf

4.1 Structure

  • Lateral, generally flattened structure borne on the stem; develops at the node and bears a bud in its axil. The axillary bud later develops into a branch.
  • Leaves originate from shoot apical meristems and are arranged in acropetal order.
  • Most important vegetative organ for photosynthesis.
  • Leaf base — attaches leaf to stem; may bear two lateral small leaf-like stipules. In monocots the leaf base expands into a sheath covering the stem. In some leguminous plants the leaf base becomes swollen = pulvinus.
  • Petiole — holds the blade to light. Long thin flexible petioles let blades flutter in wind, cooling the leaf and bringing fresh air to the surface.
  • Lamina (leaf blade) — green expanded part with veins and veinlets; middle prominent vein = midrib. Veins give rigidity and act as channels of transport for water, minerals and food.

4.2 Venation

Reticulate — veinlets form a network; generally in dicots. Parallel — veins run parallel; characteristic of most monocots.

4.3 Simple and compound leaves

  • Simple leaf: lamina entire, or incisions do not touch the midrib.
  • Compound leaf: incisions reach the midrib, breaking it into leaflets.
  • A bud is present in the axil of the petiole in both simple and compound leaves, but not in the axil of leaflets — this distinguishes a compound leaf from a branch with leaves.
  • Pinnately compound: leaflets on a common axis, the rachis (represents the midrib), e.g., neem.
  • Palmately compound: leaflets attached at a common point (tip of petiole), e.g., silk cotton.

4.4 Phyllotaxy (arrangement of leaves on stem)

TypeDescriptionExamples
AlternateSingle leaf at each node in alternate mannerChina rose, mustard, sunflower
OppositeA pair of leaves at each node, opposite each otherCalotropis, guava
WhorledMore than two leaves at a node forming a whorlAlstonia

5The Inflorescence

  • A flower is a modified shoot: the shoot apical meristem changes into a floral meristem; internodes do not elongate, the axis gets condensed, and the apex produces floral appendages at successive nodes instead of leaves.
  • When a shoot tip transforms into a flower, it is always solitary.
  • Inflorescence = arrangement of flowers on the floral axis.
FeatureRacemoseCymose
Main axisContinues to grow (indefinite)Terminates in a flower (limited growth)
Order of flowersAcropetal succession — older flowers at base, younger at topBasipetal order — older flower at top
Examples [Beyond current NCERT]Mustard, radish (raceme); wheat (spike); sunflower (head/capitulum)Solanum (cyme), jasmine, Calotropis

📌 Remember

Racemose = Acropetal (think "R-A"). Cymose = basipetal. The main axis of cymose ends in a flower.

6The Flower

6.1 General

  • The reproductive unit in angiosperms; meant for sexual reproduction.
  • Four whorls on the swollen end of the stalk (pedicel), called the thalamus or receptacle: calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium.
  • Calyx and corolla = accessory organs; androecium and gynoecium = reproductive organs.
  • In some flowers like lily, calyx and corolla are not distinct and are called perianth.
  • Bisexual — has both androecium and gynoecium. Unisexual — only stamens or only carpels.
  • Trimerous, tetramerous, pentamerous — floral appendages in multiples of 3, 4 or 5.
  • Bracteate — flowers with bracts (reduced leaf at base of pedicel); ebracteate — without bracts.

6.2 Symmetry of flower

TypeDefinitionExamples
Actinomorphic (radial)Divisible into two equal radial halves in any radial plane through the centreMustard, Datura, chilli
Zygomorphic (bilateral)Divisible into two similar halves in only one particular vertical planePea, gulmohur, bean, Cassia
Asymmetric (irregular)Cannot be divided into two similar halves by any vertical planeCanna

6.3 Position of floral parts on thalamus

TypePositionOvaryExamples
HypogynousGynoecium at the highest position; other parts below itSuperiorMustard, china rose, brinjal
PerigynousGynoecium in the centre; other parts on the rim of the thalamus almost at the same levelHalf inferiorPlum, rose, peach
EpigynousMargin of thalamus grows upward, encloses and fuses with the ovary; other parts arise above the ovaryInferiorGuava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower

📊 PYQ Trend

"Which of the following has an inferior ovary?" — guava, cucumber, sunflower (ray florets). "Half-inferior ovary" — plum, rose, peach. This list is repeatedly asked.

7Parts of a Flower

7.1 Calyx

Outermost whorl; members are sepals. Generally green, leaf-like; protect the flower in the bud stage. Gamosepalous (sepals united) or polysepalous (sepals free).

7.2 Corolla

Composed of petals; usually brightly coloured to attract insects for pollination. Gamopetalous (united) or polypetalous (free). Shapes: tubular, bell-shaped, funnel-shaped, wheel-shaped.

7.3 Aestivation

The mode of arrangement of sepals or petals in the floral bud with respect to other members of the same whorl.

TypeArrangementExamples
ValvateSepals/petals just touch one another at the margin without overlappingCalotropis
TwistedOne margin of each appendage overlaps the next one, and so on (in one direction)China rose, lady's finger, cotton
ImbricateMargins overlap one another but not in any particular directionCassia, gulmohur
Vexillary (papilionaceous)Five petals: the largest standard (vexillum) overlaps two lateral wings, which overlap the two smallest anterior petals, the keelPea, bean

7.4 Androecium

Composed of stamens (male reproductive organs). Each stamen = filament (stalk) + anther. Each anther is usually bilobed; each lobe has two chambers, the pollen sacs (so 4 pollen sacs per anther). Staminode = a sterile stamen.

TermMeaningExample
EpipetalousStamens attached to petalsBrinjal
EpiphyllousStamens attached to perianthLily
PolyandrousStamens free—
MonoadelphousUnited into one bundleChina rose
DiadelphousTwo bundlesPea
PolyadelphousMore than two bundlesCitrus
Variation in filament lengthFilaments of different lengths in the same flowerSalvia, mustard

7.5 Gynoecium

  • Female reproductive part; made of one or more carpels. Carpel = stigma, style and ovary.
  • Ovary — enlarged basal part; bears one or more ovules attached to a flattened cushion-like placenta.
  • Style — elongated tube connecting ovary to stigma. Stigma — usually at the tip of style; receptive surface for pollen grains.
  • Apocarpous — carpels free (lotus, rose). Syncarpous — carpels fused (mustard, tomato).
  • After fertilisation, ovules → seeds; ovary → fruit.

7.6 Placentation (arrangement of ovules within the ovary)

TypeDescriptionExamples
MarginalPlacenta forms a ridge along the ventral suture; ovules in two rowsPea
AxilePlacenta axial; ovules attached to it in a multilocular ovaryChina rose, tomato, lemon
ParietalOvules on the inner wall/periphery of the ovary; one-chambered ovary becomes two-chambered by a false septumMustard, Argemone
Free centralOvules on a central axis; septa absentDianthus, primrose
BasalPlacenta at the base of ovary; single ovule attachedSunflower, marigold

📌 Remember — placentation examples

Marginal — pea. Axile — china rose, tomato, lemon. Parietal — mustard, Argemone. Free central — Dianthus, primrose. Basal — sunflower, marigold.

8The Fruit

  • Characteristic of flowering plants; a mature or ripened ovary developed after fertilisation.
  • Parthenocarpic fruit — formed without fertilisation of the ovary.
  • Fruit = wall (pericarp) + seeds. Pericarp may be dry or fleshy. When thick and fleshy, pericarp is differentiated into epicarp (outer), mesocarp (middle) and endocarp (inner).

8.1 Drupe

Examples: mango and coconut. Develop from monocarpellary superior ovaries; one-seeded.

FruitEpicarpMesocarpEndocarp
MangoOuter thinFleshy, edibleStony, hard
Coconut—FibrousHard

🎯 Exam Tip

Edible part of mango = mesocarp. Coconut mesocarp is fibrous (coir). Both are drupes from a monocarpellary superior ovary.

9The Seed

Ovules after fertilisation develop into seeds. Seed = seed coat + embryo. Embryo = radicle, embryonal axis, and one cotyledon (wheat, maize) or two cotyledons (gram, pea).

9.1 Dicotyledonous seed (e.g., gram, pea, bean)

  • Seed coat — two layers: outer testa, inner tegmen.
  • Hilum — scar through which the developing seed was attached to the fruit. Micropyle — small pore above the hilum.
  • Embryo — embryonal axis + two cotyledons (often fleshy, full of reserve food). Radicle and plumule at the two ends of the axis.
  • Endospermic seeds — endosperm (formed by double fertilisation) stores food, e.g., castor. Non-endospermous seeds — endosperm absent in mature seed, e.g., bean, gram, pea.

9.2 Monocotyledonous seed (e.g., maize)

  • Generally endospermic; but orchids are non-endospermic.
  • In cereals such as maize, the seed coat is membranous and fused with the fruit wall.
  • Endosperm is bulky and stores food. Aleurone layer — proteinous layer on the outer covering of the endosperm, separating it from the embryo.
  • Embryo is small, in a groove at one end of the endosperm. It has one large, shield-shaped cotyledon called scutellum, and a short axis with plumule and radicle.
  • Coleoptile — sheath enclosing the plumule. Coleorhiza — sheath enclosing the radicle.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Coleoptile covers the plumule (shoot); coleorhiza covers the radicle (root). "Ptile" = shoot side. The aleurone layer is proteinous, not starchy.

13Plant Tissues [Beyond current NCERT]

A tissue is a group of cells having a common origin and usually performing a common function. Plant tissues are of two main types: meristematic and permanent.

13.1 Meristematic tissues

Growth in plants is restricted to specialised regions of active cell division called meristems.

MeristemLocationRole
Apical meristemTips of roots and shootsProduce primary tissues; increase length
Intercalary meristemBetween mature tissues, e.g., at the base of internodes in grassesRegenerate parts removed by grazing herbivores
Lateral meristemMature regions of roots and shoots; cylindricalProduce secondary tissues; increase girth. Examples: fascicular vascular cambium, interfascicular cambium, cork cambium

Apical and intercalary meristems are primary meristems. Lateral meristems (cambium) are secondary meristems (appear later). As the shoot apical meristem forms leaves, some cells are left behind as axillary buds, which can form a branch or a flower.

13.2 Permanent tissues

Cells that have become structurally and functionally specialised and lost the ability to divide. Simple tissues — made of one type of cell. Complex tissues — made of more than one type of cell, working together as a unit.

13.3 Simple permanent tissues

TissueCell featuresLocationFunction
ParenchymaIsodiametric (spherical, oval, round, polygonal, elongated); thin cellulose walls; closely packed or with small intercellular spacesMajor component within organsPhotosynthesis, storage, secretion
CollenchymaCells thickened at the corners due to deposition of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin; may contain chloroplastsBelow epidermis in dicots (hypodermis, petioles); absent in monocotsMechanical support to growing parts
SclerenchymaLong narrow cells, thick lignified walls; usually dead, without protoplastsFibres — various parts. Sclereids — fruit walls of nuts, pulp of guava/pear/sapota, seed coats of legumes, leaves of teaMechanical support

Fibres — thick-walled, elongated, pointed cells, usually in groups. Sclereids — spherical, oval or cylindrical, highly thickened dead cells with very narrow cavities (lumen).

13.4 Complex permanent tissues

Xylem — conducts water and minerals from roots to stem and leaves; also gives mechanical strength.

ElementFeatures
TracheidsElongated, tube-like cells with thick lignified walls and tapering ends; dead, without protoplasm
VesselsLong tube-like structures of many vessel members, lignified, large central cavity; dead; interconnected through perforations. Characteristic of angiosperms
Xylem fibresHighly thickened walls and obliterated central lumen; septate or aseptate
Xylem parenchymaLiving, thin-walled; stores food as starch or fat, and tannins

Gymnosperms lack vessels in their xylem (they have tracheids). Protoxylem — first-formed primary xylem; metaxylem — later-formed. Endarch (protoxylem towards centre) — in stems. Exarch (protoxylem towards periphery) — in roots.

Phloem — transports food materials, usually from leaves to other parts.

ElementFeatures
Sieve tube elementsLong tube-like structures; end walls perforated as sieve plates. Mature element lacks a nucleus; controlled by companion cells
Companion cellsSpecialised parenchymatous cells connected to sieve tubes by pit fields; maintain pressure gradient
Phloem parenchymaStores food, resins, latex, mucilage. Absent in most monocots
Phloem fibres (bast fibres)Sclerenchymatous; generally absent in primary phloem, found in secondary phloem. Commercial fibres of jute, flax, hemp

Gymnosperms have albuminous cells and sieve cells instead; they lack sieve tubes and companion cells.

📊 PYQ Trend

Living vs dead elements: in xylem only xylem parenchyma is living; in phloem only phloem fibres are dead. Vessels are characteristic of angiosperms; companion cells are absent in gymnosperms.

14The Tissue Systems (NCERT)

Based on structure and location there are three tissue systems: epidermal, ground (fundamental) and vascular (conducting).

14.1 Epidermal tissue system

  • Outermost covering of the whole plant body: epidermal cells, stomata and epidermal appendages (trichomes and hairs).
  • Epidermis — outermost layer, usually single-layered, parenchymatous.
  • Cuticle — waxy thick layer that prevents water loss. Absent in roots.
  • Stomata — regulate transpiration and gaseous exchange. Each stoma has two bean-shaped guard cells; in grasses, guard cells are dumb-bell shaped. Outer walls of guard cells are thin; inner walls (towards pore) are highly thickened. Subsidiary cells lie near the guard cells. Stomatal apparatus = aperture + guard cells + subsidiary cells.
  • Root hairs — unicellular elongations of epidermal cells; absorb water and minerals.
  • Trichomes — usually multicellular epidermal hairs on the stem; help prevent water loss.

14.2 Ground tissue system

All tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles: parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma. Parenchyma is present in cortex, pericycle, pith and medullary rays. In leaves, ground tissue = mesophyll.

14.3 Vascular tissue system

Complex tissues xylem and phloem together form vascular bundles.

TypeDescriptionWhere
OpenCambium present between xylem and phloem; can form secondary tissuesDicot stems
ClosedNo cambium; no secondary tissuesMonocots
RadialXylem and phloem arranged alternately on different radiiRoots
ConjointXylem and phloem on the same radius; phloem usually outer to xylemStems and leaves

15Anatomy of Dicot & Monocot Plants

15.1 Dicot root (e.g., sunflower root)

  1. Epiblema — outermost layer; many cells protrude as unicellular root hairs.
  2. Cortex — several layers of thin-walled parenchyma with intercellular spaces.
  3. Endodermis — innermost layer; single layer of barrel-shaped cells without intercellular spaces; walls have deposits of waxy suberin as casparian strips.
  4. Pericycle — thick-walled parenchyma next to endodermis. Initiation of lateral roots and vascular cambium takes place here.
  5. Pith — small or inconspicuous.
  6. Conjunctive tissue — parenchyma between xylem and phloem.
  7. Usually two to four xylem and phloem patches (diarch to tetrarch). Later a cambium ring develops.

Stele = all tissues inside the endodermis: pericycle, vascular bundles and pith.

15.2 Monocot root

Similar layers to dicot root, but more than six (polyarch) xylem bundles; pith is large and well developed; no secondary growth.

15.3 Dicot stem (young)

  1. Epidermis — outermost protective layer; thin cuticle; may bear trichomes and a few stomata.
  2. Cortex — hypodermis (collenchyma, mechanical strength) + cortical parenchyma with intercellular spaces + endodermis (starch grains = starch sheath).
  3. Pericycle — semi-lunar patches of sclerenchyma, inner to endodermis and above the phloem.
  4. Medullary rays — radially placed parenchyma between vascular bundles.
  5. Vascular bundles — many, arranged in a ring (characteristic of dicot stem); each conjoint, open, with endarch protoxylem.
  6. Pith — large number of rounded parenchyma cells with large intercellular spaces.

15.4 Monocot stem

Sclerenchymatous hypodermis; large number of scattered vascular bundles, each surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath. Large parenchymatous ground tissue (no distinction into cortex, endodermis, pericycle, pith). Vascular bundles conjoint and closed; peripheral bundles smaller than central ones; phloem parenchyma absent; water-containing cavities present.

15.5 Dorsiventral (dicot) leaf

Vertical section shows epidermis, mesophyll and vascular system. Abaxial epidermis bears more stomata; adaxial may even lack stomata. Mesophyll has two types: palisade parenchyma (adaxial, elongated, vertical) and spongy parenchyma (loosely arranged, below palisade, many air spaces). Vascular bundles surrounded by thick-walled bundle sheath cells.

15.6 Isobilateral (monocot) leaf

Similar to dorsiventral leaf, but stomata on both surfaces, and mesophyll not differentiated into palisade and spongy. Bulliform cells — large, empty, colourless adaxial epidermal cells along veins in grasses; when turgid the leaf surface is exposed, when flaccid (water stress) they make the leaf curl inwards to minimise water loss.

16Animal Tissues [Beyond current NCERT]

A tissue is a group of similar cells along with intercellular substances performing a specific function.

🔑 Key Point

All complex animals consist of only four basic types of tissues: epithelial, connective, muscular and neural. Organs (stomach, lung, heart, kidney) are made of these tissues in a specific proportion — the heart has all four types. Two or more organs performing a common function form an organ system.

16.1 Epithelial tissue

Has a free surface facing a body fluid or the outside environment; provides a covering or lining. Two types: simple epithelium (single layer) and compound epithelium (two or more layers, protective).

GlandularSpecialised cells for secretionUnicellular — goblet cells; multicellular — salivary glandSecretion
CompoundMulti-layeredDry surface of skin, moist surface of buccal cavity, pharynx; ducts of salivary/pancreatic glandsProtection

Exocrine glands — secrete mucus, saliva, earwax, oil, milk, digestive enzymes; released through ducts. Endocrine glands — no ducts; products (hormones) secreted directly into the fluid bathing the gland.

Cell junctions: Tight junctions stop substances from leaking across a tissue. Adhering junctions cement neighbouring cells together. Gap junctions let cells communicate by connecting cytoplasm for rapid transfer of ions and molecules.

16.2 Connective tissue

Most abundant and widely distributed tissue; links and supports other tissues/organs. In all except blood, cells secrete fibres of collagen or elastin plus a polysaccharide matrix (ground substance).

16.3 Muscle tissue

Made of long, cylindrical fibres composed of fine myofibrils; fibres contract then relax in a coordinated fashion.

16.4 Neural tissue

Exerts the greatest control over the body's responsiveness to changing conditions. Neurons are the excitable unit of the neural system. Neuroglial cells make up more than half the volume of neural tissue, protecting and supporting neurons. A stimulated neuron generates an electrical disturbance that travels along its membrane, triggering adjacent neurons at its ending.

📌 Remember

Tendon = muscle to bone. Ligament = bone to bone. Both are dense regular connective tissue. Blood is a connective tissue (fluid). Intercalated discs are only in cardiac muscle.

17Frog (Rana tigrina) — Morphology

17.1 General features

  • Frogs live both on land and in freshwater; class Amphibia, phylum Chordata.
  • Most common species in India: Rana tigrina (Indian bullfrog).
  • Cold-blooded (poikilotherms) — body temperature varies with the environment.
  • Change colour to hide from enemies (camouflage) — this protective coloration is called mimicry.
  • Aestivation — summer sleep; hibernation — winter sleep. They shelter in deep burrows during peak summer and winter.

17.2 External features

  • Skin smooth, slippery due to mucus; always moist.
  • Dorsal side — olive green with dark irregular spots; ventral side — uniformly pale yellow.
  • The frog never drinks water but absorbs it through the skin.
  • Body divisible into head and trunk; neck and tail absent.
  • A pair of nostrils above the mouth. Eyes bulged, covered by a nictitating membrane (protects eyes in water). Tympanum (membranous ear) on either side of the eyes receives sound signals.
  • Forelimbs end in four digits; hind limbs end in five digits, larger and more muscular, with webbed digits for swimming.
  • Sexual dimorphism — males have sound-producing vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of the forelimbs; absent in females.

18Frog — Anatomy (Organ Systems)

18.1 Digestive system

  • Alimentary canal is short because frogs are carnivores (reduced intestine length).
  • Pathway: mouth → buccal cavity → pharynx → oesophagus → stomach → intestine → rectum → cloaca (opens outside).
  • Food captured by the bilobed tongue. Liver secretes bile, stored in the gall bladder. Pancreas produces pancreatic juice.
  • Digestion in stomach by HCl and gastric juices → chyme → duodenum, where bile and pancreatic juice arrive via the common bile duct. Bile emulsifies fat; pancreatic juice digests carbohydrates and proteins.
  • Final digestion/absorption in the intestine via villi and microvilli. Undigested waste → rectum → out through cloaca.

18.2 Respiratory system

SituationOrgansType
In waterSkinCutaneous respiration (dissolved O2 by diffusion)
On landBuccal cavity, skin and lungsLungs = pulmonary respiration
During aestivation/hibernationSkin onlyCutaneous

Lungs: a pair of elongated, pink, sac-like structures in the upper trunk. Air enters: nostrils → buccal cavity → lungs.

18.3 Circulatory system

  • Well-developed closed type. Frogs also have a lymphatic system.
  • Heart — muscular; three chambers — two atria and one ventricle; covered by pericardium.
  • Sinus venosus — triangular structure joining the right atrium; receives blood via the vena cava.
  • Conus arteriosus — sac-like structure into which the ventricle opens, on the ventral side of the heart.
  • Special connections: Hepatic portal system (liver–intestine); Renal portal system (kidney–lower body).
  • Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin. Frog has single circulation (per NCERT summary).

18.4 Excretory system

  • A pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca and urinary bladder. Kidneys are compact, dark red, bean-like.
  • Each kidney has many uriniferous tubules (nephrons).
  • In males, the ureters act as urinogenital ducts. In females, ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca.
  • Frog excretes urea → ureotelic animal.

18.5 Control and coordination

  • Endocrine glands: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, pineal body, pancreatic islets, adrenals, gonads.
  • Nervous system: Central (brain and spinal cord); Peripheral (cranial and spinal nerves); Autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic).
  • Ten pairs of cranial nerves. Brain divisions: Fore-brain (olfactory lobes, cerebral hemispheres, diencephalon), Mid-brain (optic lobes), Hind-brain (cerebellum, medulla oblongata). Medulla continues into the spinal cord through the foramen magnum.

18.6 Sense organs

Touch — sensory papillae; taste — taste buds; smell — nasal epithelium; vision — eyes; hearing — tympanum with internal ears. Eyes and internal ears are well-organised structures. External ear absent; only the tympanum is visible.

18.7 Reproductive system

Male

  • A pair of yellowish ovoid testes, adhered to the kidneys by the mesorchium.
  • Vasa efferentia — 10 to 12, arise from testes, enter the kidneys and open into Bidder's canal, which communicates with the urinogenital duct opening into the cloaca.

Female

  • A pair of ovaries near the kidneys, with no functional connection to them.
  • A pair of oviducts opens separately into the cloaca. A mature female lays 2500 to 3000 ova at a time.
  • Fertilisation is external (in water). Development involves a larval stage, the tadpole, which undergoes metamorphosis into the adult.

18.8 Importance of frogs

Eat insects and protect crops; maintain ecological balance as an important link in food chains and webs; muscular legs used as food in some countries.

Frequently asked frog facts — Rana tigrina; nictitating membrane; vocal sacs and copulatory pad (male only); 3-chambered heart with sinus venosus and conus arteriosus; nucleated RBCs; ureotelic; urinogenital duct in males only; 10 pairs of cranial nerves; Bidder's canal; vasa efferentia 10–12; 2500–3000 eggs; hepatic and renal portal systems.
